Tyre Pressure and Fuel Use

Many people find tyre pressure and fuel use confusing at first, because the numbers look small on their own and the effect only becomes clear over weeks or months. That is exactly why a clear method matters more than a quick guess. When you write down your starting point, the figure you want to reach, and the time you have available, the calculation stops being a mystery and becomes a plan you can check. This guide walks through that method step by step, using plain language and worked examples with real numbers, so you can repeat the same calculation with your own figures. By the end you will know which inputs matter most, which common mistakes push the answer in the wrong direction, and how to adjust the plan when life changes.

How it works

The calculation behind tyre pressure and fuel use follows a simple idea: take the quantity you use, multiply it by the price or rate that applies to it, and add any fixed charge that applies whether you use a little or a lot. The hard part is not the multiplication, it is finding the right quantity and the right rate. Start by gathering your real figures from a bill, a meter, a receipt or your own records, and write them down before you open the calculator. Then enter them one at a time, checking each one as you go. If a figure is an estimate, say so to yourself and try the calculation twice, once with a low estimate and once with a high one, so you can see the range of possible answers instead of trusting a single number.

Worked example

Under-inflated tyres can raise consumption by a few percent. A car using 8 litres per 100 km that rises to 8.4 litres uses 0.4 litres more per 100 km, costing 1.00 more per 100 km at 2.50 per litre, or 10 more per 1,000 km.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake with tyre pressure and fuel use is entering a figure in the wrong units, for example a yearly amount where a monthly amount was expected, or a price that includes tax where a price without tax was needed. The second common mistake is forgetting a fixed charge, a standing fee, or a one-off cost that is small on its own but adds up over a year. The third is using a rate found online as if it were your own rate, when rates vary by country, provider, plan and time of year. Always check your own bill or contract for the rate that applies to you, and treat any figure in a guide as an example unless it comes from your own provider. Label your examples clearly and keep a note of where each figure came from.

  • Mixing up monthly and yearly figures in the same calculation.
  • Using a rate from another country or provider as if it were your own.
  • Forgetting a fixed charge, a fee, or a one-off cost that applies every period.
  • Rounding at every step instead of rounding only the final answer.
Tip: Check tyre pressure monthly, when the tyres are cold.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out my fuel cost for a trip?

Multiply the distance by your fuel consumption, then multiply by the price per litre or per gallon. The trip calculator on this site does this for you, using the units you enter.

What affects fuel consumption most?

Speed, traffic, load, tyre pressure, air conditioning and driving style all affect consumption. Smooth driving at a steady speed usually uses less fuel than frequent acceleration and braking.

Is an electric car cheaper to run?

It often is per kilometre, because electricity usually costs less per unit of energy than petrol, but the answer depends on your local fuel and electricity prices. Enter your own prices in the comparison tool.

Putting it into practice

The best next step is a small one. Open the calculator on this site, enter your real figures, and save or note the result. Then set a date, perhaps a month from now, to check the result against what actually happened. That single check will teach you more about your own usage than any guide can, and it will show you exactly which input to improve next. Keep the method, keep the notes, and adjust the plan when the numbers change, and tyre pressure and fuel use will stay under control instead of surprising you.
