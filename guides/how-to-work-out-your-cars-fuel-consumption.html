How to Work Out Your Car's Real Fuel Consumption

Getting working out real fuel consumption right is mostly about being honest with your inputs. A calculator can only work with the numbers you give it, so a hopeful estimate in and a hopeful answer out is the usual pattern. This guide helps you choose realistic inputs, understand what each one does to the final figure, and build a small safety margin into your plan. It covers the basic method first, then a worked example with real numbers, then the mistakes people make most often, and finally a short set of questions and answers. Read it once with your own numbers beside you, and you will have a plan you can trust and adjust.

How it works

The method for working out real fuel consumption is straightforward once you see it as a sequence rather than a single sum. Begin with the amount you start from, then add what goes in, subtract what goes out, and apply the rate or price to what remains. Do that in the same order every time, and check the units at each step: litres with litres, kilowatt-hours with kilowatt-hours, gigabytes with gigabytes, and months with months. A unit mistake is the most common cause of an answer that is ten or a hundred times too large. If your answer looks surprising, check the units first, then check whether you entered a yearly figure where a monthly figure belonged, and only then question the method itself.

Worked example

Fill the tank, note the mileage, drive, then fill again. If you used 40 litres over 500 km, consumption is 8 litres per 100 km. If you used 45 litres over 500 km, it is 9 litres per 100 km.

Common mistakes to avoid

A third set of mistakes comes from copying a result from one situation into another. A figure that is right for a small household may be wrong for a large one, a rate that applies in one country may not apply in another, and a plan that worked last year may not work after a price change. Re-check your figures at least once a year, and whenever a bill, a price or your usage changes. Keep the old calculation beside the new one so you can see what changed and by how much. That habit turns a one-off calculation into a useful routine, and it catches small errors before they become expensive ones.

  • Mixing up monthly and yearly figures in the same calculation.
  • Using a rate from another country or provider as if it were your own.
  • Forgetting a fixed charge, a fee, or a one-off cost that applies every period.
  • Rounding at every step instead of rounding only the final answer.

The most reliable check is the brim-to-brim method, where you fill the tank, note the distance driven, then fill again and divide the distance by the fuel used.

Tip: Measure over at least three fill-ups; one tank is too noisy to trust.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out my fuel cost for a trip?

Multiply the distance by your fuel consumption, then multiply by the price per litre or per gallon. The trip calculator on this site does this for you, using the units you enter.

What affects fuel consumption most?

Speed, traffic, load, tyre pressure, air conditioning and driving style all affect consumption. Smooth driving at a steady speed usually uses less fuel than frequent acceleration and braking.

Is an electric car cheaper to run?

It often is per kilometre, because electricity usually costs less per unit of energy than petrol, but the answer depends on your local fuel and electricity prices. Enter your own prices in the comparison tool.

Putting it into practice

To finish, remember the three habits that make working out real fuel consumption easy over time: use your own figures, check the units, and re-check after a month of real use. Do those three things and the calculator becomes a planning tool rather than a source of surprises. Share the method with anyone in your household who shares the bill or the budget, so everyone is working from the same numbers, and review the plan together whenever a price or a habit changes.
